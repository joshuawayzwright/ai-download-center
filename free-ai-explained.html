Explainer · Free AI tools

Free AI tools come in three kinds. Here’s what each one really costs you.

“Free” is the most common word in AI tool marketing, and it means at least three different things. A free plan on a hosted service, a free app that runs on your own computer and open-source software each ask something different of you: limits, hardware or setup time. Knowing which kind you’re looking at tells you what you’re trading away before you start.

By AI Download Center Published 19 November 2026 Facts checked 19 November 2026 7 min read

Key points

  • Free plan: start in a browser in a minute, but the maker sets the limits and can change them, and your prompts go to its servers.
  • Free local app: no subscription and your data can stay on your machine, but your computer does the work.
  • Open source: you get the code and a license, not a promise of free usage. Some open-source tools still need a paid model or account.

1. Free plans on hosted services

This is what most people mean by a free AI tool: open a website or app, sign in and start typing. The model runs on the maker’s servers, so your computer doesn’t matter, but everything you type is sent to that company. Read its privacy terms before pasting in anything confidential.

The trade-off is control. The maker decides how much you can use the free plan, which models it includes and when the limits change. Our catalog labels these tools “Freemium”: a free tier exists, and paid plans add more.

2. Free apps that run on your own computer

Local apps download an AI model to your machine and run it there. Once the model is on your disk there’s no subscription, and your prompts don’t have to leave your computer. The cost moves to your hardware: models take gigabytes of storage, and how fast they run depends on your processor, graphics card and memory.

Models small enough to run on a typical laptop are usually less capable than the largest hosted models, so expect some trade between privacy and capability. Our local LLM apps guide compares more options.

LM Studio

Desktop tool for downloading, running, and testing local models offline.

Who it suits: Beginners who want a desktop app with a model browser, not a terminal.

What we checked: LM Studio says it is “free to use both at home and at work”[5]; its terms limit that to personal and internal business use, and rule out reselling it as a hosted service.[6] Its docs say it can run entirely offline once you have downloaded model files.[7]

3. Open-source tools

Open source means the code is published under a license that lets you read, run and change it. It says nothing on its own about whether using the tool is free. An open-source coding agent is only as free as the model you connect it to, and open-source models often ask you to install and configure them yourself.

The payoff is control: you can keep and run the version you have, read exactly what it does, and decide which services, if any, it connects to.

Aider

Terminal-based AI coding assistant for local workflows and code changes across repositories.

Who it suits: Developers who work in the terminal and want AI to edit code across a repository.

What we checked: Aider is licensed under Apache 2.0.[12] It needs a language model: you either add your own API key for a hosted model, so any usage charges come from that provider[13], or connect a local model, for example through Ollama.[14]

Whisper

Speech recognition model for transcription, multilingual indexing, and audio analysis.

Who it suits: People comfortable installing software from the command line who want to transcribe audio on their own machine.

What we checked: Whisper is MIT-licensed.[15] Its README says it works with Python 3.8 to 3.11 and needs the command-line tool ffmpeg installed.[16]

The fine print: three cautionary cases

Three tools in our catalog show how far the labels can drift from what a reader might assume.

  • Open source, but no longer free to use: Gemini CLI. Google’s terminal agent is open source, yet its README now says it has “no free tier”: free individual access moved to Google’s Antigravity CLI on 18 June 2026.[17] We removed it from our free tools starter kit on 19 November 2026 for that reason.
  • Free and source-available, but not a standard open-source license: Open WebUI. Its custom “Open WebUI License” follows a BSD-3-style template but adds a clause that prohibits removing or altering the Open WebUI branding, with exceptions that include deployments serving 50 or fewer users in a rolling 30-day period.[18] Our catalog lists it as “Free (source available)” rather than open source.
  • Free, but check whether it’s still maintained: GPT4All. The most recent commit to its main branch on GitHub is dated 27 May 2025.[19] Free software that no longer gets updates can fall behind on models and security fixes.

How to choose

  • You want to try AI today and have nothing to install: start with a free plan, such as ChatGPT, Claude or Perplexity.
  • You handle confidential material, or want no subscription: try a local app such as LM Studio or Jan, and check your computer can run the model you pick.
  • You write code: GitHub Copilot Free works inside common editors. Aider is open source, but budget for the model you connect, or run one locally. See our AI coding assistants guide.
  • You need transcripts and are comfortable with a command line: Whisper runs on your own machine.
